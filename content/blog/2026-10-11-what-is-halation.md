**What is halation?** It's the red-orange glow that forms around bright lights on film: streetlights, neon signs, the sun glinting off a car. Light punches through the film, bounces back from behind it, and exposes the emulsion a second time right around the highlight. The result is that warm, slightly smeared halo people now associate with cinema and night photography.

It's also one of the hardest film traits to fake well on a phone, so it's worth understanding what's actually going on.

## What is halation in photography?

Film is a stack of very thin layers. When light from a bright source hits it, most of that light is absorbed where it lands, but some passes all the way through. That stray light reflects back toward the emulsion and exposes it again, a little to the side of where it first landed. Do that around a strong point of light and you get a glowing ring.

Exactly where the light bounces back from depends on who you ask. Analog.Cafe's explainer argues the main culprit is total internal reflection at the boundary between the back of the film base and the air behind it. Dehancer's write-up describes light reflecting off the camera's inner surfaces. The mechanism is the same either way: light escapes the emulsion and comes back.

Halation happens in black-and-white film too. Without colour layers, it simply shows up as a white glow instead of a red one. Glow is one of three things behind [the black and white film look](/blog/black-and-white-film-look), alongside grain and how film turns colour into grey.

## Why the glow is red (and sometimes orange)

Colour film records blue, green and red light in separate layers. The blue-sensitive layer sits closest to the lens, then green, and the red-sensitive layer is the deepest, nearest the back of the film.

So when light reflects back from behind the film, the first layer it meets on the return trip is the red one. That's why the halo reads as red. When the reflection is especially strong, it reaches the green layer too, and the mix shifts the glow toward orange. In practice you often see orange right at the edge of a blown-out highlight, fading to red further out.

Analog.Cafe also notes a subtler side effect: in colour film, halation can tint very bright areas slightly, so a bright overcast sky can lean pinkish.

## Anti-halation layers, and the film that skips them

Most modern films are designed to suppress halation. They include an anti-halation layer that absorbs light before it can bounce back. On motion-picture film, Dehancer explains, this is a black carbon backing, needed because movie cameras don't have the dark pressure plate a stills camera does. The layer isn't perfectly black, so a faint halo can still appear around very strong lights.

The film everyone points to for halation does the opposite. **CineStill 800T** is made from motion-picture stock with its remjet backing removed, which lets regular labs develop it in standard C-41 chemistry. With the absorbing layer gone, bright point lights glow red: lamps, signs and reflections on wet streets in particular, as Unique Photo's profile of the film describes. That's why so many night-street photos have that unmistakable look.

## Halation vs bloom vs lens flare

These three often get lumped together as "glow", but they come from different places and look different:

| Effect | What you see | Where it comes from |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Halation | A warm red or orange halo hugging the edge of bright highlights | Light scattering and reflecting inside the film |
| Bloom | A softer, neutral white glow where bright areas spill into their surroundings | Diffusion: soft lenses, filters, haze or fog |
| Lens flare | Streaks, starbursts or circles across the frame | Direct light bouncing between elements inside the lens |

The quick test: if the glow is colourful and tight to the light source, it's halation. If it's white and soft, it's bloom. If it has shapes and streaks pointing away from the light, it's flare. Real photos often show more than one at once.

## How halation is recreated digitally, and how to spot a fake

Software can't bounce light around inside a strip of film, so digital halation is a model of the effect. Grain works the same way: [digital grain is designed noise](/blog/film-grain-vs-digital-noise), and the good versions follow the image. The good ones follow the physics. Dehancer's halation tool, for example, lets you set which brightness levels count as a light source, how far the halo spreads, how much it leans toward orange, and keeps bloom as a separate control.

Here's what separates a convincing version from a sticker:

1. **It only appears around the brightest highlights.** Real halation needs a strong light. A glow on every edge, including mid-grey ones, is a filter, not halation.
2. **The colour comes from the light.** Red to orange, strongest near the source. A flat orange wash over the whole image isn't it.
3. **It scales with brightness.** A bare bulb should halate more than a lit window, and a lit window more than a white wall.
4. **It's separate from bloom.** Halation and bloom can appear together, but they're different effects with different colours.
5. **It's subtle in daylight.** On film, halation is easiest to see at night, against dark backgrounds. A daylight portrait drowning in red glow has been overcooked.

If you want to see it for yourself, shoot point lights at night: streetlamps, string lights, a neon sign. Keep the background dark so the halo has something to stand out against. One catch on a phone: HDR processing pulls bright lights down to keep detail, which flattens exactly the highlights that should glow.
