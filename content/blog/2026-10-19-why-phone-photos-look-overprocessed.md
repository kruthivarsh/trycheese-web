Phone photos often look overprocessed because the camera never takes just one picture. It merges several frames, lifts the shadows, pulls the highlights down, then sharpens and smooths the result so it pops on a small screen. That's **why phone photos look overprocessed**: flat light, crunchy edges, skies with every cloud and none of the glow.

You can't switch all of it off. But 2026 phones give you more control than they used to, and a few habits help on any camera.

## Why phone photos look overprocessed

Phone sensors are small, so the software does a lot of heavy lifting. Apple's own description of HDR is a good summary of what every flagship does: the camera takes several photos in rapid succession at different exposures and blends them to bring more detail into the highlights and shadows. By default it does this whenever it judges HDR most effective.

That merge is genuinely clever, and it's why a phone can shoot into a sunset without losing the foreground. The side effects are what make photos feel clinical:

- **Everything lands in the middle.** Tone mapping drags bright areas down and dark areas up, so contrast flattens out and nothing is allowed to be truly dark.
- **Edges get crunchy.** Sharpening makes detail read at thumbnail size, and leaves halos where a roofline meets the sky.
- **Texture turns waxy.** Noise reduction scrubs away speckle, the digital cousin of film grain, and takes skin texture, foliage and fabric with it.
- **Lights lose their glow.** Bright lamps and the sun get pulled down to keep detail, so the highlights that should bloom sit there politely instead.

None of that is a bug. It's tuning for a photo that looks impressive at a glance. If you want something closer to a film look, the work is mostly in undoing those choices.

## iPhone: what you can and can't turn off

**Smart HDR.** Apple's guide only offers a manual HDR switch on iPhone 11 models, iPhone SE (2nd generation) and iPhone 12, under Settings > Camera. On anything newer there's no toggle: HDR is automatic.

**Photographic Styles.** Styles adjust colour and tone in different parts of the frame and apply to every photo you take. Open Camera, swipe up from the modes, tap Styles, then Customize (on recent models) to change tone, colour and intensity. Apple notes you need High Efficiency (HEIF) capture, set in Settings > Camera > Formats, to edit the style later in Photos. Styles shape the colour. They don't switch off the merging underneath.

**Texture and Grain on iPhone 18 Pro.** New in iOS 27, the Customize panel adds a Texture control with Soft Skin, Glow and Film options, plus a Grain control that adds film grain on top. As MacRumors reported on 22 September 2026, Apple first described wider support, then narrowed it: the controls now apply only to photos taken on iPhone 18 Pro models (and some cameras on the iPhone Duo).

**ProRAW.** Shooting RAW feels like the obvious escape, but Apple is clear that ProRAW combines standard RAW data with iPhone image processing, and it works with Smart HDR, Deep Fusion and Night mode. It gives you far more room to edit. It's not unprocessed. Turn it on in Settings > Camera > Formats (iPhone 12 Pro and later Pro models), and budget for roughly 25 MB per 12 MP photo or 75 MB at 48 MP.

**A single-frame camera app.** If you want the merge gone entirely, a third-party camera has to bypass Apple's pipeline. Lux's Halide has a mode called Process Zero that takes one frame, uses no computational photography and develops the photo from sensor data, aiming for what Lux calls "beautiful, film-like natural photos". Lux is upfront about the trade-offs: softer, less saturated, grainier files, dynamic range limited to the sensor, and noisy results in low light with no night mode.

## Pixel: Camera Looks and RAW

Google's big change this year is **Camera Looks** on the Pixel 11, Pixel 11 Pro, Pixel 11 Pro XL and Pixel 11 Pro Fold. The Original Pixel look stays the default, but you can swap it for one of three alternatives: Natural, Shadows or Vanilla. Google describes Natural as relaxed on detail, Shadows as deeper contrast and Vanilla as a warm, golden glow.

The interesting part for anyone tired of the HDR look: Google says it built these partly by merging fewer frames for a less processed feel, and partly by rewriting colour lookup tables to echo film chemistry. Six more stylised Looks sit alongside them (Digi, Black Tie, Minimal, Editorial, Classic and Velvet), and each can be fine-tuned with sliders like Contrast and Highlights.

To set a new default, open the Camera in Photo mode, tap Controls at the bottom right, then Looks at the bottom left, and pick from the dropdown at the top. Looks don't work in Video mode.

On older Pixels, RAW is the main lever. In the Camera, tap Settings > More settings > Advanced and turn on RAW/JPEG control (Pixel 8 Pro skips this step), then turn on RAW + JPEG in Photo mode settings. You get a RAW file next to the usual JPEG, to edit your own way.

## Samsung Galaxy: Camera Assistant

Samsung hides its most useful processing controls in an add-on called Camera Assistant, downloaded from the Galaxy Store and opened from Camera settings > Camera Assistant. Samsung's guide (written for the Galaxy S23 Ultra) includes two settings worth changing:

1. **Auto HDR.** Turn it off to stop the camera evening out bright and dark areas.
2. **Picture softening.** Switch it on to smooth over-sharp edges and textures in Photo mode.

Options vary by model and One UI version, so check what your Galaxy actually offers.

## Habits that help on any phone

Settings only get you part of the way. These work whatever you shoot on:

- **Expose for the highlights.** Tap to focus, then drag exposure down a touch. Exposure compensation leaves the camera less to rescue, and shadows stay shadows.
- **Pick contrasty light on purpose.** Side light and late sun give the image shape. Flat midday light invites the camera to flatten it further.
- **Stay on the real lenses.** Digital zoom leans harder on processing to rebuild detail. Use 1x or the phone's actual telephoto.
- **Edit down, not up.** If a photo already looks crunchy, lower sharpening and clarity before you touch anything else.
- **Don't paint film on top of HDR.** A filter over a heavily merged photo looks like a sticker. That's the difference we break down in our guide to [film camera apps on Android](/blog/film-camera-apps-for-android-2026), where the look is part of the capture instead.
